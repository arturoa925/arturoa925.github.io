# A Crock-Pot, not a microwave

My AI setup is a Crock-Pot, not a microwave.

A microwave is one chatbot doing everything. It's fast, but the results come out mediocre. A Crock-Pot does the slow work in the background while I go about my day. I come back, finish it off, and adjust the seasoning myself.

That's how my bots work. Each one has one job, and none of them overlap. They do the heavy lifting. I make the final calls.

I used to be against AI assistance. I use it every day now, with one rule: **AI is a lever and a tool. It is not an opponent.** It speeds the work up. It does not decide what "done" means.

## One bot, one job

One chatbot doing every job averages them, so each result comes back a little wrong. One role per bot is more efficient. It only has to be good at one thing, and I only have to review one thing.

**Design** works on screens, layout, and how a flow should feel. It does not build what it draws.

**Code Master** writes the implementation after I've decided what it should do: boilerplate, tests, bugs, and UI changes. It does not choose the direction.

**Job Hunter** stays on the job search. It can gather roles and draft. It does not decide which roles fit, or describe my work in a way the repos don't support.

**Portfolio Bot** drafts pages for this site. It does not publish them. A sentence I can't explain doesn't go up.

**Media bot** drafts the short public version of a claim I already settled. It does not add claims, and it does not decide that something is ready to post.

None of them share a job. That narrowness is what makes the automation useful. A bot with one assignment can run while I do something else. A bot doing everything needs me watching the whole time.

## What runs while I'm away

I hand a bot one job and the context that job needs, then I leave. A layout, a pass through a project, a stack of roles, a page, a short post: that slow work doesn't need me in the thread. I go about my day, and it keeps going.

I come back to a draft, not a finished result. I read it until I can explain it, cut anything that wandered into a second job, and change what doesn't sound like me or doesn't match the work. The background run is the efficiency. The seasoning is still mine.

## A tool, not an opponent

People worry that AI will replace them, or that using it will take their skills away. That happens if you hand over the decisions. If the bot picks the direction and you only accept the result, you stop building judgment. The bot gets better at answers. You get worse at knowing which ones are right.

I use it as a lever. The bots multiply the first pass: the layout tries, the drafts, the search, the repetitive edits. They do not multiply my taste. I still have to know what good looks like, because I'm the one who sends work back. That review is practice. [Arguing with the result](/writing/arguing-with-ai) is how the skill stays sharp. Accepting everything is how it dulls.

A lever doesn't fight the person holding it. It only helps if someone is still holding it. The bots do a job I named. I decide whether the job was done.

## The risk

The setup fails if I stop coming back. A draft that reads smoothly is not a draft I checked. Taking it would be faster, and it would mean the tool made the call I said I would make.

So I come back and finish it. The Crock-Pot did the slow part. I still decide what ships.
